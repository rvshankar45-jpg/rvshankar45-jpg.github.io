How this works, in one minute

Companies don't tell you what they fixed — three of these four describe every update as “bug fixes and improvements”. So this study ignores what they say and asks a different question: did people stop complaining?

I collected 325,715 public Google Play reviews, kept the 43,649 that were complaints, and sorted each into one of four categories — price, product, delivery, app. Jev AI did that sorting for the hardest cases and taught a local classifier to handle the rest, reaching 91.4% accuracy on reviews it had never seen.

Then each category was tracked release by release, counted as complaints per 1,000 reviews so a bigger app isn't unfairly penalised. “Fixed” was defined in advance as an arithmetic rule — a 40% drop off a peak, held for two releases — so no AI decides what counts as progress. Separately I measured how often each company replies, how fast, and how much those replies repeat themselves.

Full method near the end, including everything that went wrong.

First, what people complain about

Every complaint in this study was sorted into one of four categories. They're deliberately broad, because a category has to hold enough reviews for a trend to mean anything. Here is exactly what goes in each one.

PRICE — what it costs
Fees, charges and money owed back.
delivery fees · handling & platform charges · GST · surge pricing · offers that didn't apply · refunds never credited
PRODUCT — what arrived

Getting the order to the door.

A fifth bucket, other, holds complaints about customer support itself and reviews that are just anger with no stated cause (“worst app”). It's kept separate rather than forced into the four, and it's about a quarter of everything — which is a finding in itself.

What each app's users actually complain about

The rankings say who has more complaints. This says what kind. Each app has a recognisable personality in its reviews — and users name the specific thing that annoys them with surprising precision.

The food mostly arrives fine — Zomato has the lowest product complaint rate of all four. Two things that cut across all four

  • Everyone is angry about unbundled fees, but each brand has its own villain. The shared phrases are “extra charges” and “handling charges”. The distinctive ones are brand-specific: surge for Blinkit, cancellation for Swiggy, platform for Zomato, handling for Zepto. Users have learned the vocabulary of each company's pricing page and are quoting it back.
  • The “app” category is barely about software. Its top phrases are cash available, cod option, cancel option — payment methods and missing buttons, not crashes. These are product-policy decisions that users experience as the app being broken.

The experiment nobody meant to run

Two of these companies made opposite bets on answering reviews. One replies to essentially everything; the other to essentially nothing. If replying achieved anything measurable, this is where it would show.

Outcome: the same. Both flat. Measured across all twenty app-and-category pairings, how much a company replies has no detectable relationship with whether its complaints go down.

A fiftyfold difference in effort produced a difference too small to see.

And the replies are machines

Two-minute reply times made me suspicious, but speed alone proves nothing — a well-staffed team can be fast. What proves it is that the speed never changes. A human team queues: reviews left at 3am wait for the morning shift.

Twenty-two times as many reviews arrive at 9pm as at 5am. The reply time doesn't budge — under a minute of variation across the entire day, each app locked to its own number. That's a setting, not a shift rota. Blinkit even has a floor: not one reply faster than five minutes, then a tight cluster at 10–12, which is what a scheduled job looks like.

How this was done

The whole approach rests on one idea: don't ask companies what they fixed — ask whether people stopped complaining. Release notes were the obvious alternative and they're useless, because three of the four say only “bug fixes and improvements”.

Collect the reviews, politely

325,715 public Google Play reviews for the four apps, newest first, pausing between requests. Reviewer names and profile pictures were discarded on arrival and never stored — the analysis never needed them.

Keep only the complaints

One- and two-star reviews, plus three-star reviews that read as complaints rather than lukewarm praise. That left 43,649 complaints inside the study window.

Sort them into the four categories

A few thousand of the hardest reviews were labelled by Jev AI, and those labels trained a classifier that sorted the rest. It was then tested against reviews it had never seen: it agrees with the reference 91.4% of the time, against 26% for guessing the most common category.

Decide what counts as “fixed” — before looking

A category counts as fixed if its share of complaints drops at least 40% off a peak and stays down for two or more app releases. It's an arithmetic rule, not a judgement call, so the same data always gives the same verdict. No AI decides what “fixed” means.

Measure as a rate, not a share

Complaints per 1,000 reviews. Shares alone can't tell “nothing improved” apart from “everything got worse together” — a fixed denominator can.

Measure the replies separately

How often each company replies, how fast, how much the replies repeat themselves, and crucially whether reply time changes by hour of day — which is what exposed the automation.

Try to break the result

Every threshold was re-run at looser and tighter settings, and the ranking re-computed on two different date ranges. The conclusions held. Where a check failed, it's in the limitations below rather than quietly dropped.

Where Jev AI was used — and where it wasn't Jev AI (TypeSafe AI) is a decision API: you hand it a situation and a fixed set of options, and it picks one and tells you how confident it is. It did exactly two jobs here. One: deciding whether an ambiguous three-star review was really a complaint — 4,080 of those. Two: sorting the hardest complaints into the four categories. Rather than run all 43,649 through it, the local classifier flagged the ones it was least sure about and only those were escalated, twice over. About 13% of complaints were labelled by Jev directly; the other 87% inherited that judgement through the classifier trained on it, which is what lifted accuracy from 62.6% to 91.4%.

Deliberately not used for two things. It did not invent the four categories — a model's idea of what people complain about is not evidence about what these people complained about. And it never decided whether something was “fixed”: that stays an arithmetic rule on counts, so the central claim of this piece is reproducible without any AI at all. Every reply from Jev was cached, so re-running the analysis costs nothing and returns identical answers.
Where the machine got overruled The original plan was to let an algorithm discover the complaint categories by itself. It failed, and interestingly. It grouped 2,391 Zomato reviews together purely because they mentioned Zomato, and built another group entirely out of Hinglish grammar words — filing Hinglish price complaints alongside Hinglish delivery complaints. Five more groups were just intensity (“worst app”, “3rd class service”), which is a mood, not a category. So the four categories here were written by a human, using the algorithm's output as evidence rather than as the answer.

What would make me wrong

  • This shows association, not cause. A flat complaint rate after a release doesn't prove the release changed nothing. Seasonality, a change in when the app asks for reviews, or a shift in who reviews would all look identical.
  • Three months is short. Eight to twelve releases per app. A fix that works slowly would read as no fix here. Google Play would not serve older reviews however long I kept asking.
  • Zepto is only partly included. It has 39 days of history — too few releases to judge whether anything improved. Its complaint rates and reply figures are in; its fix verdicts are not.
  • Reviewers aren't users. Angry people write reviews far more often than satisfied ones. This describes the complaining tail, not the typical experience — Zepto's 143 partly reflects who it prompts to review.
  • The sorting is 91.4% accurate, so roughly one complaint in eleven sits in the wrong category. That matters for the exact numbers; it matters much less for the trends, since a steady error rate largely cancels out when comparing one release to the next.
  • No individual was tracked. Reviewer names were deliberately discarded, so “did this same person complain again” can't be answered. What I measured is whether the same kind of complaint kept arriving at the same rate.

So what

If you run a product, the uncomfortable reading is that answering reviews and resolving them are two separate systems that don't talk to each other. One is automated and hits 99% coverage in minutes. The other is invisible in the data.

The reply is doing something — it's reputation management, aimed at the next person reading the store page, not at the person who complained. But as a measure of how responsive a company is, reply rate tells you nothing about whether problems get solved.

If the line is flat for three months while you reply to 99% of complaints, the replies aren't the intervention you think they are.

The more useful instrument is unglamorous: pick a handful of complaint categories, measure each as a rate per 1,000 reviews, and watch them across releases.

And check your own pipeline before you trust it. Nearly everything interesting here came from a number that looked wrong — an average reply time of “0.0 hours” that turned out to be minutes, a reply that appeared to arrive three years before the review, a 400-review sample that was quietly only 75, and a night shift that never existed.
